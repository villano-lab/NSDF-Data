Data and setup

We are using the file 07221203_2025_F0001, which is series 07221203_2025, Dump 1. The first 1000 events are “random” triggers (the “Beginning of Run Randoms,” BORR, taken at about 20 Hz: 1000 events in 49 seconds); random triggers are snapshots of our detector traces taken at any time (rather than when a pulse occurs) to sample the noise environment. The line in the third code block of the noise-psd-randoms-buddy.ipynb notebook shows the randoms cut defined as quiet_noise = pc.excursion_band(pulses, config_500) & (np.log10(bstd_500) < 0.5) in addition to selecting the trigger specification for random events. The other 8 of the 1008 events that carry the randoms label are “in-run” randoms: they are spread through the rest of the dump, one every 1 to 2 seconds (all 8 in the last 9 seconds of the dump; the timestamps have one-second resolution). The run configuration of series 07220916_2200 lists 5 Hz for in-run randoms, which does not match what we see here, so that rate should not be assumed for this series.

Note. The trigger-type labels in the dump’s .csv are the wrong way round. The column trigger_type has two values, Physics and Unknown. By their names one would read Physics as an event triggered by a pulse and Unknown as a trigger of undetermined type. In this dump it is the opposite: the first 1000 events, which are the Beginning of Run Randoms (the run configuration asks for 1000 randoms at 20 Hz, and these 1000 events span 49 s, which is 20.4 Hz), are all labelled Physics, and the other events, 509 in this dump, are labelled Unknown. So we take Physics to mean a random trigger and Unknown to mean a real (pulse) trigger, which is what pc.randoms and pc.real_triggers do. This is a working assumption: nothing in the data files or the DAQ documentation we have confirms it, and the two functions are tagged under development for that reason (see Note 2a).

It may also be useful to have a diagram of the detector and which channels are working during the R76 run period at UMN. The figure below was reconstructed from the archive of the UMN Wiki.

How we found which channel is which. The data are numbered by detector (0 to 2) and channel (0 to 3), but there is one detector, S104, read out by three four-channel electronics boards (DCRC1, DCRC3, DCRC4). We matched them to the detector’s channels in four steps:

  1. The channel table. The Setup table of the UMN Run 76 summary lists the 12 channels by board, with the detector channel in its Label column (Side1pA to Side2pF). We used that column. The HV columns repeat entries and look like typos.
  2. The board order. The table does not say which board is detector 0, 1 or 2. The raw file of another Run 76 series (07220916_2200; the raw file for series 07221203_2025, the one analysed here, is not on NSDF) holds the run configuration. It enables boards DCRC1, DCRC3 and DCRC4, and the three records of each event carry the codes 9, 11 and 12, which are the board numbers plus 8. So detector 0, 1, 2 = DCRC1, DCRC3, DCRC4. This is inferred and not confirmed.
  3. The channels within a board. We assumed they follow the table’s row order. The data agree: the dead channels, whose signal never changes (channel 2 of DCRC1 and DCRC3, and channels 1 to 3 of DCRC4) are the ones that configuration lists with zero QET bias.
  4. The faces. The wiki’s source-position figure draws Side 2 as seen after flipping the detector over, which is how the figure below shows it.

The mapping is kept in python/pulse_channels.py, marked as under development, and section 7 of that notebook, noise-psd-randoms-buddy.ipynb, gives the evidence.

The question

Based on this series’ Beginning of Run Randoms (BORR), what is our best estimate for the noise environment of the experimental setup? The noise environment is typically summarized in terms of the Power Spectral Density (PSD).

Selection of Randoms in All Channels

Typically, BORR are taken in the first events of the first “dump” (file) of a series’ raw data. In this series that is the case and there are 1000 BORR. Often some of the initial traces are “contaminated” with a real physics pulse that just happened to be in the random’s readout window. We call this a “pileup” pulse. Those occurances can mess up the noise computation and therefore the extracted pulse amplitudes; pulse amplitudes are used to estimate the energy deposited in each event. Here I describe how we make a cut on the data to be sure those pileups are removed.

This sort of thing has been done on one channel, PCS2, and it is the topic of Note 2 and Note 2a. Note 2 develops the noise-pulse selection: an excursion cut on a 500-sample pretrigger window (the Tight band) together with a cut on the baseline scatter, log10(bstd) < 0.5, which keeps 179 of the 1517 traces. Note 2a adds the trigger cut that keeps only the randoms, 141 of those 179 (the other 38 are real triggers), and looks at how the baseline scatter changes with time.

To use that cut I have to decide how to apply it to all of the channels. A pileup should show up in all channels simultaneously, because the origin is a real physics event. It is possible the amplitude in some channels could be small enough so that it’s not obvious. Figure 2 is a plot based on the cut for PCS2 only (see the Data and setup section). To my slight surprise I found that this cut does not remove some traces that are either glitches or tails of pulses on Side 1. I don’t want those pulses in the noise sample.

Appendix B shows the traces left in S1 after the PCS2 cut (Figure 7), and then the S1 cut itself (Figure 8). To my surprise there were not traces in S2 that were very apparent. That means that for some reason only cutting on S1 is more pure. Since only cutting on S1 is also less restrictive than the combined cut by definition, this led to more accepted randoms (101, compared with 71 for one channel on each side and 31 for all channels).

We will use this cut going forward: the cut of Notes 2 and 2a (a random trigger in the Tight excursion band with log10(bstd) < 0.5) made on PBS1 (S1). In the notebook it is defined in one place, clean_randoms_cut, and the selection it makes is called clean_randoms.

Noise Power Spectral Densities

The key parameter we want to get out of pulses in these detectors is the overall “amplitude” because it should be proportional to the energy deposited in a scattering event. That’s hard to do for the small signals (near the noise baseline) that we have for dark matter direct detection (the expected recoil spectrum rises roughly exponentially toward low energy, with no turnover; Lewin and Smith [1]) so we use an algorithm called the Optimal Filter (OF) (Gatti and Manfredi [2]; Golwala [3]; Press et al. [4]; Szymkowiak et al. [5]) instead of a simpler procedure like integrating the pulses. The crucial part of the OF is that it takes into account the electronic noise environment of the sensor, rather than blindly integrating. The noise is summarized by the Power Spectral Density (PSD) constructed with traces that have no pulses present–the ones we are selecting here.

The PSD is sort of like the standard deviation of different frequency components in a trace (any trace can be written as a sum of sinusoids of different frequencies, the Fourier decomposition [4]). The PSD can be calculated for discrete data sampling as below, by averaging the squared Fourier amplitudes of many traces (Welch [6]):

Take a trace of NN samples x[n]x[n], n=0,…,N−1n = 0, \ldots, N-1, taken every Δt\Delta t (here Δt=1.6μs\Delta t = 1.6\,\mu\mathrm{s}), with its baseline subtracted. Its discrete Fourier transform is X[k]X[k], with k=0,1,…,N/2k = 0, 1, \ldots, N/2 (NN is even here), which are the non-negative frequencies, all that the one-sided PSD needs. Bin kk sits at the frequency f[k]=k/(NΔt)f[k] = k/(N\,\Delta t), from zero (k=0k = 0) up to the Nyquist frequency 1/(2Δt)1/(2\,\Delta t) (k=N/2k = N/2). Averaging over MM traces with no pulse in them (here the M=101M = 101 traces of the clean_randoms cut, or the 31 of Appendix A), the one-sided PSD is

Xm[k]=∑n=0N−1xm[n]e−2πikn/N,k=0,1,…,N/2,S[k]=2ΔtN⋅1M∑m=1M|Xm[k]|2(counts2/Hz), \begin{aligned} X_m[k] &= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_m[n]\, e^{-2\pi i\, k n / N}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, N/2, \\ S[k] &= \frac{2\,\Delta t}{N}\cdot\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left|X_m[k]\right|^2 \qquad \left(\mathrm{counts}^2/\mathrm{Hz}\right), \end{aligned}

where xm[n]x_m[n] is trace mm and Xm[k]X_m[k] its transform. The amplitude spectral density is ASD[k]=S[k]\mathrm{ASD}[k] = \sqrt{S[k]}, in counts/Hz\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}. The factor 2 folds the negative frequencies onto the positive ones (it is left out for the zero-frequency and Nyquist bins), and the 1/N1/N and Δt\Delta t make SS independent of the trace length and the sampling rate, so that its integral over frequency equals the mean square of the trace (its variance, if the trace has zero mean). Here NN = 4086, the length of a trace after the leading glitch samples are dropped, so there are 2044 bins, from 153 Hz (the first non-zero bin) to 312 kHz; the zero-frequency bin is not plotted. This is the convention used in the notebook (noise-psd-randoms-buddy.ipynb); Note 3 plotted the raw |X[k]|2|X[k]|^2 instead, which differs from SS only by the constant 2Δt/N2\,\Delta t/N.

Conventions. We use one-sided spectra: the PSD S[k]S[k] above, in counts²/Hz, and the amplitude spectral density (ASD) S[k]\sqrt{S[k]}, in counts/Hz\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}, which is the quantity the figures show and the usual way to quote noise. Golwala [3] (appendix B, pp. 409 to 418) uses a different convention in three ways. His Fourier transform has the factor 1/N1/N on the forward transform (p. 412), so his ṽn\tilde{v}_n is our X[k]/NX[k]/N. His PSD J(f)J(f) is two-sided, with ∫−∞∞J(f)df\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} J(f)\,df equal to the variance (p. 415). He estimates it as J=2T|ṽ|2J = 2T\,|\tilde{v}|^2, averaged over noise traces, with 2T=NΔt2T = N\,\Delta t (p. 417); the averaging over traces is the same as ours. In our terms J[k]=(Δt/N)|X[k]|2=S[k]/2J[k] = (\Delta t/N)\,|X[k]|^2 = S[k]/2 for 0<f<fNyquist0 < f < f_{\mathrm{Nyquist}}, so his amplitude J\sqrt{J} is our ASD divided by 2\sqrt{2}. He also notes that the quantity usually quoted, J\sqrt{J}, is often wrongly called the PSD (p. 415). The difference matters when the spectrum goes into the optimal filter, whose expected resolution is σA2=[∫−∞∞|s̃(f)|2/J(f)df]−1\sigma_A^2 = \left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\tilde{s}(f)|^2/J(f)\,df\right]^{-1} for a pulse template s̃\tilde{s} (his equation B.1, p. 418). Written with our one-sided SS it is σA2=[4∫0∞|s̃(f)|2/S(f)df]−1\sigma_A^2 = \left[4\int_{0}^{\infty} |\tilde{s}(f)|^2/S(f)\,df\right]^{-1}, and using SS in his formula unchanged would be off by a factor of 2. The template s̃\tilde{s} must use his scaling of the Fourier transform too.

Figure 4 shows the amplitude spectral density of the 101 events of the clean_randoms cut, the cut used from now on. Above 100 kHz it is flat, between 2.37e-3 and 2.59e-3 counts/√Hz in five of the seven channels; the narrow lines of the DCRC3 channels PES1 and PES2 raise their means to 3.65e-3 and 4.07e-3 counts/√Hz. PBS2 is the odd one: between 1 and 10 kHz its amplitude is 2.61e-3 counts/√Hz, against 6.71e-3 to 1.23e-2 for the others (see Caveats).

Caveats

  • PBS2 (DCRC4) may not be a normally operating phonon channel. Its noise is flat and well below the other working channels at low frequencies (between 1 and 10 kHz its amplitude is 2.61e-3 counts/√Hz, against 6.71e-3 to 1.23e-2 for the others; Figure 4), and it passes the quiet-noise cut for 958 of the 1008 randoms, against 101 to 180 for the others. This could mean the channel is superconducting or otherwise not biased into its transition. The UMN wiki does not say so: it never mentions superconducting channels, and its only related remark is that three channels (pB, pD, pE, with no side given) “had railed in the middle of the data period” in October. The run configuration we read for another series (07220916_2200) lists zero QET bias on all four DCRC4 channels, with the feedback of channel 0 disabled, which fits a channel that is not in transition but does not prove it. Until this is confirmed, the spectra of PBS2 should not be taken as a phonon noise measurement.

Results

The cut: clean_randoms

The selection of randoms that we use from now on is called clean_randoms. It is the cut of Notes 2 and 2a, made on PBS1 (S1): a random trigger (pc.randoms) whose trace falls in the Tight excursion band of the 500-sample pretrigger window (pc.excursion_band) and has log10(bstd) < 0.5. In series 07221203_2025, dump F0001 it keeps 101 of the 1008 randoms, none with a pulse or a big tail in any channel (Appendix B). It is defined by the function clean_randoms_cut in chan0-cut-on-all-channels-buddy.ipynb (section 13), and the code below is that function. clean_randoms is a True/False value for every event of the dump; because the three boards share their events in the same order, the same list selects the clean random traces of every channel.

import sys
from pathlib import Path
import numpy as np
from nsdf_dark_matter.idx import load_all_data

REPO = next(p for p in [Path.cwd().resolve(), *Path.cwd().resolve().parents] if (p / "python" / "pulse_io.py").exists())
sys.path.insert(0, str(REPO / "python"))
import pulse_io as pio
import pulse_quantities as pq
import pulse_cuts as pc
from pulse_config import PulseConfig

config = PulseConfig(pretrigger_samples=500, glitch_samples=10, sample_period_s=1.6e-6)

def clean_randoms_cut(pulses, trigger_types, config=config):
    """Randoms with no pulse: a random trigger in the Tight excursion band with log10(bstd) < 0.5."""
    with np.errstate(all="ignore"):
        quiet = pc.excursion_band(pulses, config) & (np.log10(pq.bstd(pulses, config)) < 0.5)
    return pc.randoms(trigger_types) & quiet

cdms = load_all_data(str(Path.home() / "idx" / "07221203_2025_F0001"))
det1 = pio.filter_by_detector(cdms.get_detector_ids(), 1)
_, pulses = pio.load_channel_batch(cdms, det1, 1)               # Side1pB: detector 1, channel 1
clean_randoms = clean_randoms_cut(pulses, pio.trigger_types(cdms, det1))   # True for the events of the cut
print(clean_randoms.sum(), "of", len(clean_randoms), "events")

det0 = pio.filter_by_detector(cdms.get_detector_ids(), 0)
_, other = pio.load_channel_batch(cdms, det0, 0)                # any other channel: same events, same order
traces = other[clean_randoms]                                   # its clean random traces
print(traces.shape)

Run it from inside the NSDF-Data folder, after downloading the dump to ~/idx. It prints 101 of 1517 events and (101, 4096). The library prints a warning that pc.randoms is under development, because it assumes the trigger labels are swapped (Note 2a).

The PSDs

I’ve made the current best estimate of the noise spectrum specifically focusing on the series 07221203_2025. The spectra are accessible via psd-clean-randoms-07221203_2025_F0001.h5 file (pinned to 618687c, 503 kB), and can be accessed by:

import h5py
import numpy as np

with h5py.File("data_analysis/R76/student/buddy/psd-clean-randoms-07221203_2025_F0001.h5", "r") as f:
    freq = f["frequency_hz"][:]                       # Hz, shared by all channels
    events = f["event_numbers"][:]                    # the 101 clean_randoms events
    asd = {name: (g["asd_mean"][:], g["asd_sem"][:])  # counts/sqrt(Hz), one-sided, and its standard error
           for name, g in f["channels"].items()}

mean, sem = asd["Side1pB"]
print(len(events), "events;", len(asd), "channels:", ", ".join(asd))
print(f"Side1pB at {freq[100]:.0f} Hz: {mean[100]:.2e} +/- {sem[100]:.1e} counts/sqrt(Hz)")

Run it from the NSDF-Data folder. It prints the number of events and the 7 working channels (Side1pB, Side1pE, Side1pF, Side2pB, Side2pC, Side2pE, Side2pF: PBS1, PES1, PFS1, PBS2, PCS2, PES2 and PFS2 in the notation above); the dead channels have no spectrum. For each channel the file holds the amplitude spectral density of the 101 events of the clean_randoms cut (asd_mean, one-sided, in counts/√Hz) and its standard error (asd_sem), and the PSD it is the square root of (psd_mean and psd_sem, in counts²/Hz); the layout of the file is described in section 7 of psd-clean-randoms-buddy.ipynb, which also makes them. The caveats above apply, in particular to PBS2.

Next steps

Next steps. There are two possibilities:

  1. Calibrate the PSD in amps. Get the calibration for the PSD so that we can write the noise in amps, and later compare it with the theoretical best resolution. This will probably be difficult, because it is not clear that the UMN wiki has the necessary information. As far as we found, the DCRC v3 page gives the size of one ADC bin (14 bits over ±1.25 V, 152.6 µV per bin) but not the feedback resistor, the SQUID coil ratio or the TES bias power, which are needed to turn counts into amps.
  2. Find triggered pulses. Find triggered pulses in dump 2 or later, so we can continue on the path of running the Optimal Filter on pulses.

Appendix A: the strictest cut (every working channel)

The strictest version of the selection keeps a random only if it passes the quiet-noise cut (the Tight excursion band and log10(bstd) < 0.5) in every working channel. Seven of the 12 channels work and five are dead (their signal never changes), so the cut is applied to the seven. It keeps 31 of the 1008 randoms, far fewer than the 141 that pass on PCS2 alone. Each of the six working channels on DCRC1 and DCRC3 passes the cut for only 101 to 180 randoms on its own (DCRC4’s PBS2 for 958), and the events that fail are not the same in all of them. Any two of those six channels share 62% to 89% of the smaller set (well above the 11 to 29 events expected by chance), but each further channel removes more, so only 31 events pass in all of them, 31% of the 101 of PBS1, the strictest channel. The Tight excursion band does most of the rejecting: only 116 to 189 of the 1008 randoms fall in the band on each of those six channels, while the log10(bstd) < 0.5 condition keeps 770 to 895.

Figure 5 shows the 31 traces themselves in every channel (section 12 of chan0-cut-on-all-channels-buddy.ipynb): none of them has a pulse or a ramp, and they all look like noise, but there are only 31 of them. Figure 6 shows their noise amplitude spectral density, the mean of the 31 traces in each channel (section 11 of noise-psd-randoms-buddy.ipynb). With so few traces the spectra are jagged from bin to bin, and no spread between the traces is drawn. This is why a cut that is not required to hold on all channels, and so keeps more traces, is worth looking for.

Appendix B: the cut on PCS2 and the cut on PBS1

What the PCS2 cut lets through. Of the 141 randoms that pass the cut on PCS2, 10 have a pulse or a big tail in some channel: 2 contain a pulse and 8 more have a baseline sag of more than 30 counts across the trace. Figure 7 shows only these 10, each in its own colour, which is the same in every tile. Event 10550 (light green) steps up by about 273 counts in PFS1 (at sample 1139) and by about 359 counts in PES2 (at sample 1171), falls back at sample 1219 in both, and steps up again at samples 1323 and 1355. Event 10034 (light blue) is a long decay in the three DCRC3 channels, down to about -100 counts. The big tails are in DCRC3, and the sag is largest in PBS1 for 8 of the 10 events (up to 133 counts for event 10027). In the framed PCS2 tile, and in PBS2 on DCRC4, the same 10 traces lie in the same noise band as all the others, which is why the PCS2 cut cannot see them.
